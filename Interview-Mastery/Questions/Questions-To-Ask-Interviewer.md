# Questions To Ask Interviewer

## Questions

1. What are the main responsibilities of this role?
2. What kind of backend systems will I work on?
3. What is the main technology stack used by the team?
4. Is the architecture monolithic, microservices-based, or hybrid?
5. What are the biggest technical challenges the team is solving now?
6. What are the expectations in the first 3 months?
7. How does the team handle code reviews?
8. How does the team handle deployments?
9. What CI/CD tools are used?
10. How are production issues monitored?
11. What observability tools are used?
12. How is ownership divided across services?
13. Does the team follow Agile/Scrum?
14. How much backend vs frontend work is expected?
15. Are there opportunities to work on system design and architecture?
16. How does the team handle technical debt?
17. How are performance issues handled?
18. What databases and messaging systems are used?
19. What cloud platform is used?
20. What does success look like for this role?

---

## Answers

1. What are the main responsibilities of this role?
   - **Answer:** This question anchors every other question in the conversation because it defines the day-to-day reality behind the job description. The job posting may describe the role in broad marketing language, but the interviewer will reveal what the position actually demands: hands-on coding, architecture reviews, on-call duties, mentoring, or cross-team coordination. Ask this early, right after the interviewer's own opening pitch, so the rest of the discussion can build on a shared understanding. The phrasing should stay simple and open, such as "To make sure I have an accurate picture, what does a typical day or week look like in this role?" Pay attention to whether the answer emphasizes delivery speed, ownership, collaboration, or breadth, because that reveals what the team genuinely values and rewards. If the interviewer hesitates or gives only a vague summary, that may indicate a role whose scope is not well defined, which is itself important information. Compare the answer against the job description; any major mismatch is a red flag worth probing further. This question also signals engagement, because it demonstrates that the candidate is thinking about fit rather than simply collecting an offer. A thoughtful follow-up could be to ask which responsibility takes up the most time, which surfaces the real priority of the position.

2. What kind of backend systems will I work on?
   - **Answer:** This question moves the conversation from generic responsibilities to the concrete engineering reality: the systems, their scale, and the kind of problems the role will actually confront. Understanding whether the work involves data-intensive pipelines, high-throughput APIs, batch processing, or internal tooling lets the candidate judge whether the problems align with their interests and growth goals. It is best asked after the responsibilities question, because it naturally deepens that earlier answer. A good phrasing is "Can you describe the systems the team works on and the scale they operate at?" Listen for signals about data volume, request rates, latency requirements, and how many users or services depend on those systems. An interviewer who can describe concrete scale and specific system names is likely telling the truth; one who stays vague may be describing a less mature or less interesting environment. The response also indicates how much greenfield work exists versus maintaining and extending legacy systems, which directly affects the learning curve and the kind of impact a new hire can have early on. Asking this shows seniority, because junior candidates tend to ask about tasks while experienced candidates ask about systems and the problems those systems create.

3. What is the main technology stack used by the team?
   - **Answer:** This is a practical compatibility question that clarifies how much of the day-to-day work will leverage existing skills versus require new learning. It matters less for judging the "best" technology and more for understanding ramp-up time and whether the candidate will enjoy the work. The best moment to ask is after systems have been described, so the stack can be discussed in the context of what it actually supports. Phrase it as "What is the primary stack the team builds on, and are there newer tools you are starting to adopt?" Note that there are two parts to any good answer: the languages and frameworks used today, and the direction the team is heading. An interviewer who mentions recent migrations or tool evaluations is giving a signal about where the platform is going, which helps the candidate judge whether their skills will be relevant in a year. If the stack is unfamiliar, that is not automatically a reason to reject the role, but it should prompt follow-up questions about how much learning time is expected and whether there are systems that still use the candidate's strongest technologies. This question also invites conversation about tooling standards, which often reveals how much engineering discipline the organization practices.

4. Is the architecture monolithic, microservices-based, or hybrid?
   - **Answer:** Architecture is the single strongest predictor of the kinds of engineering problems a team faces, so this question deserves a prominent place in the interview. A monolith typically means simpler operations but bigger releases, harder ownership boundaries, and potentially heavy code-review friction; microservices bring distributed-systems complexity, debugging challenges, and ownership questions; hybrid arrangements often reflect an organization mid-transition. Ask this question mid-conversation, after the stack discussion, and phrase it neutrally: "How would you describe the current architecture, and what is the direction the team is heading?" The last part matters as much as the first, because a team moving from monolith to services faces migration problems, while a team consolidating services faces integration problems. If the interviewer cannot clearly describe the architecture, that is a meaningful signal about documentation and architectural awareness. If the architecture is a monolith, ask whether there are plans to break it apart and on what timeline, which reveals whether the candidate would be joining the migration itself or simply operating within it. Understanding architecture also lets the candidate anticipate which skills will be most valuable: transactional consistency for monoliths, distributed tracing and eventual consistency for microservices.

5. What are the biggest technical challenges the team is solving now?
   - **Answer:** This question reveals whether the team is solving interesting problems or merely keeping the lights on, and it is one of the most revealing questions in the entire interview. Ask it in the middle of the conversation, after basic context is established, so the interviewer has enough shared background to give a meaningful answer. A good phrasing is "What is the hardest technical problem the team is working through right now?" The response exposes the team's priorities, pain points, and the level of engineering maturity, whether they are fighting performance bottlenecks, data consistency issues, scaling problems, or platform instability. Compare what the interviewer describes against the candidate's own strengths; if the problems align, that can be acknowledged directly as a point of interest. A candidate who can respond to the described challenge with relevant questions shows depth and immediately demonstrates that they are thinking at the systems level rather than the task level. This is also a low-risk question to ask any interviewer, because it does not require prior knowledge and it positions the candidate as genuinely curious about the work. If the interviewer struggles to name a single real challenge, the team may be operating in reactive mode, which is a subtle but valuable signal.

6. What are the expectations in the first 3 months?
   - **Answer:** This question converts the abstract promise of a role into a concrete onboarding plan, and it signals that the candidate thinks about impact from day one. Asking about the first three months shows the interviewer that the candidate is results-oriented and already picturing themselves doing the work. It is best asked toward the end of the interview, once the role is clear, and phrased as "What would you hope I have accomplished in the first three months?" The quality of the answer reveals how well the team has thought about onboarding: strong teams describe specific deliverables, ramp-up milestones, and the people a new hire will work with, while weaker teams offer generic phrases like "get up to speed." Pay attention to whether the expectations are realistic and whether there is a named mentor or structured review process, because that indicates whether growth is actively managed or left to chance. The answer also clarifies whether the first months are about shipping code quickly or about learning the domain before contributing, which helps the candidate set their own expectations. A mismatch between the stated expectations and the role responsibilities can be probed further to uncover hidden priorities.

7. How does the team handle code reviews?
   - **Answer:** The code review culture is one of the most accurate indicators of engineering quality, so this question deserves a careful answer and careful listening. Ask it in the context of engineering practices, after the stack and architecture questions, and phrase it as "How do code reviews work on the team, in practice?" The phrase "in practice" is deliberate: many teams describe an ideal review process that does not match what actually happens. Listen for signals such as whether reviews are required before merging, whether they catch real bugs or just style nits, and how long reviews typically take to come back. Teams with a healthy review culture will describe specific practices such as pairing on hard changes, reviewing for performance and security, and having constructive rather than competitive discussions. The answer also reveals how much a new hire will learn: thorough reviews are a learning mechanism, while rubber-stamp reviews mean isolated development. If the interviewer cannot describe the review process concretely, that is a signal that reviews may not happen consistently. This question positions the candidate as someone who values quality and collaboration, which are traits every engineering leader wants to hear.

8. How does the team handle deployments?
   - **Answer:** Deployment frequency and process reveal a great deal about the team's confidence in its pipeline and its appetite for risk. Teams that deploy daily or multiple times per day have invested in automation, good testing, and fast rollback; teams that deploy monthly are often burdened by manual processes and fear of change. Ask this mid-interview, grouped with the CI/CD questions, and phrase it as "How often does the team deploy, and how does a change get from a developer's machine to production?" The answer should cover the mechanics, such as rolling updates, blue-green, or canary strategies, and the culture, such as whether a failed deploy causes a postmortem or a blame game. A candidate who asks about deployment strategy demonstrates production awareness, which is a hallmark of senior engineers. If deployments are manual, ask what the plan is to automate them, because that reveals whether the team is investing in its platform or only shipping features. The response also hints at on-call load: frequent, well-instrumented deployments tend to reduce the drama of individual releases, while rare, stressful deployments suggest a more chaotic operational environment.

9. What CI/CD tools are used?
   - **Answer:** This is a practical tooling question that clarifies whether the candidate's pipeline experience transfers directly and whether the team treats automation as a first-class concern. It is best asked alongside the deployment question, and phrased as "What does the CI/CD pipeline look like, from commit to production?" Beyond the specific tools, listen for what the pipeline actually does: whether it runs unit and integration tests, performs static analysis, builds artifacts, and can be extended by engineers without special access. Teams with mature pipelines will describe gate stages, environment promotion, and infrastructure-as-code; immature teams may say the tool name and nothing else. The depth of the answer is itself the signal, because it reveals whether the team has invested in engineering velocity or treats the pipeline as a black box. This question also surfaces whether the team practices trunk-based development or long-lived branches, which affects how changes are integrated and reviewed. A candidate who asks about pipeline details shows that they care about the entire delivery lifecycle, not just the code they write, and that is exactly the attitude a senior hire is expected to bring.

10. How are production issues monitored?
    - **Answer:** This question reveals how the team detects, responds to, and learns from failures, which is central to the health of any production system. Ask it toward the end of the engineering section of the interview, and phrase it as "When something goes wrong in production, what happens?" The ideal answer covers detection through dashboards and alerts, response through on-call rotations and escalation paths, and reflection through blameless postmortems that produce actionable changes. Listen for whether monitoring is proactive or reactive: mature teams describe alerting on meaningful thresholds, while less mature teams describe discovering problems from customer complaints. Also ask about the on-call rotation itself, because that is a real part of the job: how often it rotates, whether there is compensation or time-off-for-on-call, and whether the whole team participates. This question is essential for setting expectations, because some engineers enjoy owning operations while others prefer to focus purely on development. The way the interviewer describes incidents also reveals cultural values: teams that mention postmortems and follow-up actions are demonstrating a learning culture, while teams that mention "who broke it" are demonstrating a blame culture.

11. What observability tools are used?
    - **Answer:** Observability is the backbone of effective debugging, so this question reveals whether the team can actually investigate problems efficiently or whether they are flying blind. Ask it after the monitoring question, since the two are related, and phrase it as "How does the team get visibility into system behavior, including logs, metrics, and traces?" A complete observability stack covers structured logging, metrics collection, and distributed tracing, and teams that have all three can answer questions about production quickly and confidently. If the interviewer can name specific tools and describe how they are used, that signals real operational investment; if the answer is vague or limited to a single tool, the candidate should consider whether debugging will be a constant struggle. This question also matters for onboarding, because learning an unfamiliar observability stack takes real time and affects how quickly a new hire can become productive. Asking about traces in particular shows a sophisticated understanding of distributed systems, which is a differentiator in an interview. The response can also hint at how the organization thinks about reliability overall, which is the larger question the candidate actually wants answered.

12. How is ownership divided across services?
    - **Answer:** This question clarifies the candidate's future scope: whether they will own specific services end to end, share responsibility across the whole platform, or work as a generalist wherever needed. Ownership is one of the strongest predictors of both career growth and daily satisfaction, so it is worth asking directly. Phrase it as "How does the team divide ownership across the services, and what would a new hire own?" A team that assigns clear service ownership tends to produce engineers with deep system knowledge and real accountability, while a shared-everything model can leave responsibility ambiguous and support load uneven. The answer also reveals team size relative to the surface area they support, which indicates how much load each engineer carries. If services are owned by individuals, ask how cross-service coordination happens, because that affects how often the candidate must navigate dependencies and negotiate interfaces. This question signals that the candidate is thinking about accountability and long-term system health rather than just completing assigned tickets. It also helps the candidate judge whether they will get a recognizable domain they can become expert in, which is important for both credibility and advancement.

13. Does the team follow Agile/Scrum?
    - **Answer:** Process questions are less about the specific framework and more about cadence, planning, and how priorities are set. Most teams use some form of Agile, but the reality varies enormously, from disciplined sprints with real ceremonies to agile-in-name-only environments where the process is paperwork. Ask this in the culture and workflow section of the interview, and phrase it as "How does the team plan and prioritize its work, and what does a typical sprint look like?" The answer reveals sprint length, the quality of planning meetings, whether retrospectives lead to real change, and how technical debt and infrastructure work are scheduled against feature work. Listen for whether the process serves the engineers or the engineers serve the process: teams that describe flexible ceremonies and empowered planning are generally healthier than teams that describe rigid rituals. This question also touches on how the candidate's work will be scoped and reviewed, which sets expectations for how much autonomy they will have. It is a standard but important question, and asking it thoughtfully shows that the candidate understands how engineering organizations operate and can adapt to a team's rhythm.

14. How much backend vs frontend work is expected?
    - **Answer:** This question protects against the most common source of role mismatches: a job described as backend that is actually full-stack or frontend-heavy in practice. Ask it directly and early, especially if the job description is ambiguous, and phrase it as "What is the rough balance between backend and frontend work in a typical week?" The answer should be specific, and if it is not, ask what the last few weeks actually looked like. Beyond the split, the question reveals whether the team is well-resourced on the frontend or expects every engineer to cover everything, which changes the daily reality of the role. A candidate who prefers backend work should be alert to answers that mention occasional frontend tasks repeatedly, since those small exceptions can grow into a substantial portion of the job. It is also worth asking about the codebase composition, because a heavily frontend codebase constrains how much backend work there can even be. This question signals that the candidate has clear career direction and is choosing the role deliberately rather than accepting anything, which recruiters and hiring managers respect. Getting a clear answer here prevents a difficult conversation months into the job.

15. Are there opportunities to work on system design and architecture?
    - **Answer:** This question establishes whether the role offers a path beyond implementation into design and architecture, which is a key factor in long-term growth as a senior engineer. It is best asked in the growth and development portion of the interview, and phrased as "How do engineers on the team get involved in design and architectural decisions?" Listen for concrete mechanisms: design documents, architecture reviews, technical forums, or rotation onto infrastructure projects. A team that has explicit structures for involving engineers in design will describe them naturally, while a team without them will give an uncertain answer. The response also reveals whether the candidate would be working on greenfield design or predominantly operating within established patterns, which shapes how much they will learn. If design opportunities exist only for senior engineers, ask what it takes to get there, because that defines the realistic timeline and path. This question signals ambition and ownership thinking, traits that interviewers associate with candidates who stay and grow rather than those who churn. It also lets the candidate judge whether the role will stretch them or just repeat their existing level of work.

16. How does the team handle technical debt?
    - **Answer:** How a team treats technical debt reveals its engineering values more honestly than any stated mission or value. Ask this in the workflow discussion, and phrase it as "How does the team balance shipping features against paying down technical debt?" A healthy answer describes a deliberate process: debt is tracked, prioritized, and given dedicated time, whether through refactoring sprints or rules such as fixing what is touched. A poor answer is a defensive justification for permanently cutting corners, which means quality will keep eroding while the candidate is there. The answer also reveals the team's realistic autonomy: whether engineers can pause feature work to fix a problem they found, or whether every decision is dictated from above. Listen for examples, because a team that names a specific piece of debt and how they are handling it is far more credible than one that gives a generic principle. This question signals that the candidate cares about sustainable software, not just short-term delivery, which is exactly the attitude teams want in someone who will own code long term. It is also useful for setting expectations about how much of the candidate's time will be spent on unglamorous maintenance.

17. How are performance issues handled?
    - **Answer:** This question clarifies whether performance is treated as an afterthought or as a first-class engineering concern, which determines how much of the job will involve optimization work. Ask it after the architecture and monitoring questions, and phrase it as "When the team hits a performance problem, how does it typically get investigated and resolved?" The ideal answer describes a process: measuring and profiling to find the real bottleneck, testing changes against baselines, and following up with regression protection. Listen for whether they have dedicated performance testing, load testing, and the tooling to understand bottlenecks, because teams that lack this are reduced to guesswork when things get slow. The response also reveals whether performance is considered during design and code review or only after customers complain. A candidate with strong performance skills should note whether that expertise would be valued and used, and every candidate should note whether the environment would be pleasant to debug in. This question demonstrates engineering maturity, because it shows the candidate understands that performance is a system property, not a single fix, and that they care about how the team handles it.

18. What databases and messaging systems are used?
    - **Answer:** This is a compatibility and scale question: the specific databases and messaging systems in use reveal the data volumes, consistency requirements, and architectural patterns the candidate will work with daily. Ask it alongside the stack and architecture questions, and phrase it as "What data stores and messaging systems are core to the platform, and at what scale are they operated?" Beyond the names of the technologies, listen for how they are used: whether the database is the source of truth, whether Kafka or a similar system handles event flows, whether there are caches, and how data is replicated. The answer also reveals whether the candidate would be working on data-intensive problems they find interesting or spending their time on simple CRUD operations. If the technologies are unfamiliar, that signals a learning investment worth quantifying; if they are familiar, the candidate can ask deeper questions about partitioning, throughput, and failure handling to demonstrate depth. This question also connects to growth, because exposure to sophisticated data infrastructure is one of the fastest ways to build senior-level skills. It shows the interviewer that the candidate thinks in terms of the whole data path rather than just the code they will write.

19. What cloud platform is used?
    - **Answer:** The cloud platform shapes everything from deployment strategy to cost management to how much infrastructure work engineers do themselves. Ask this with the tooling questions, and phrase it as "What cloud platform is the team on, and how much infrastructure responsibility sits with the engineers versus a platform team?" The answer reveals whether the candidate will be provisioning infrastructure, working with managed services, or leaving operations to a dedicated team, and each of those shapes the role very differently. Listen for how deeply the platform is integrated: whether they use managed databases, serverless components, container orchestration, or largely virtual machines, because that defines the architectural vocabulary of the team. The response also hints at the organization's cloud maturity, including how they handle cost, security, and multi-region availability. If the platform differs from what the candidate knows, the question opens a natural discussion about learning paths and whether that transition is supported. This question signals that the candidate thinks about the full operating environment of their software, not just the code, and that they understand cloud architecture is part of the job.

20. What does success look like for this role?
    - **Answer:** This is the single most important closing question in the interview, because it reveals how performance will actually be judged and what the candidate needs to achieve to be considered a strong hire. Ask it near the end, once the conversation has established context, and phrase it as "Looking at the first six months to a year, what would success in this role look like?" The quality of the answer reveals whether the team has clear expectations: strong teams can describe measurable outcomes, key projects, and behaviors that define success, while weak teams offer vague phrases like "hit the ground running." Listen for whether success is defined by delivery speed, code quality, ownership, or business impact, because that tells the candidate what to prioritize from day one. This answer also sets the foundation for future discussions about reviews and promotions, so capture it carefully. A candidate who asks this question signals that they are serious about performing, not just getting hired, which leaves a strong final impression. It is also the candidate's last chance to spot a mismatch between the promise of the role and the reality, making it both a strategic and a defensive question.
